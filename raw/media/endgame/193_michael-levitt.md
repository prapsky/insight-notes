**[00:02:16] Gita Wirjawan:** Hi. Today we're honored to have Professor Michael Levitt, who is a professor of structural biology at Stanford University. Michael is also a Nobel laureate; he won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2013. Michael, thank you so much for being here.

**[00:02:30] Michael Levitt:** Thank you so much for having me. It's a real pleasure to be here.

**[00:02:34] Gita Wirjawan:** Michael, you were born in Pretoria, grew up in South Africa, went over to the UK, and then went over to Israel, and now you're at Stanford. You've been here since 1987. What made you and how did you get curious about science, and what would it take to win a Nobel?

**[00:02:49] Michael Levitt:** Firstly, the very simple answer is good luck. Many, many people are deserving of the Nobel Prize, and it depends on what they're interested in, what they want to focus on, and so on. So I think becoming a Nobel laureate is an unpredictable process. On the other hand—and we can say more about this later—the consequences of becoming one are much more deterministic.

**[00:03:16] Michael Levitt:** I think I was just a very curious kid. A mother who actually lived until 107—she passed away a year and a half ago—who was forced to leave school young, but she had a brother who went to university in South Africa. Poor family, so they couldn't afford to educate her; she was the third child. He did very, very well in science, and I think she felt that as her firstborn, educating me was really important. Well, she never made me feel that way, and my childhood in South Africa was filled with being a kid, playing around, having friends, and probably being way more interested in girls and dancing and things like that at the age of 12 than I was in science.

**[00:04:05] Michael Levitt:** And then again, I see that my whole life is full of unintended consequences of various effects. Something that really got me going was coming home at night when I was 15 at 4:00 a.m. having been playing snooker with my friends. My friends at school were always the least good students because they were the most fun. I did fine; I was not the best student in the class, but I was probably in the top five, but I didn't put a lot of effort into it.

**[00:04:37] Michael Levitt:** My mother basically said, 'Look, firstly I've been really worried because South Africa is a dangerous place, and you didn't let me know, and I was staying up all time. But clearly, you aren't interested enough in school. Why don't you just skip school?' I was now 2 years before the end of school. Schooling in South Africa is actually a relatively low level—it's way lower than the schooling in Britain. So basically we were just coming up to the summer holidays (which are winter here), and she basically said, 'Look, you should skip, try to finalize your matriculation in the next 3 months, and then go to university, and then we can see what you're going to do.'

**[00:05:22] Michael Levitt:** I kind of was intrigued by this. She put a lot of money—and she didn't have much money, she'd been recently divorced—in private lessons. The trouble was matriculation isn't just math and physics; it's also English and Afrikaans and Latin and history, subjects that I wasn't particularly good in. But I had the private lessons, and I managed to pass—essentially passing the exam, which was like the backup exam from matriculation. Most people took it before the summer, but if they failed, they had a second chance.

**[00:05:49] Michael Levitt:** She then got me accepted into Pretoria University, which was actually an Afrikaans-speaking university in Pretoria. They wouldn't accept me at Wits University because I was too young. I basically went there for a year, and then as a reward, she said, 'Why don't you go and visit London?' I had never been out of the country, so I flew to London by myself, stayed with my uncle and aunt who were both very, very good scientists, and that was the start of everything.

**[00:06:21] Michael Levitt:** I decided not to go back to South Africa. I went to King's College at a relatively young age—I was 17 when I went in. The thing was that being at university in South Africa for a year was equivalent to one year of A-levels in England, so basically I had to go to a technical college to get my A-levels. I got them.

**[00:06:50] Michael Levitt:** And then another lucky circumstance: in South Africa, there hadn't been television, and I saw television in London and was totally addicted to it. I remember this television about this size, black and yellow because there wasn't really any white in those days. By pure chance, a Nobel laureate, John Kendrew, who had won the Nobel Prize at the end of 1962, had a television program that came on in January 1964. Of course, there's only one channel, so I watched it. It was called 'The Thread of Life', and it was basically a beautiful introduction to molecular biology, actually at a pretty high level. I saw this and I said, 'Wow, that's what I want to do.'

**[00:07:33] Michael Levitt:** Now in England, if you have A-levels, you can't get into Oxford or Cambridge unless you do an extra year of S-levels. But King's College had been implicated in the whole DNA story, so I thought, 'This is a college in London; I love London, it's a great place to be.' I went to King's College for my BSc degree in physics—not chemistry, not biology, because physics is easy. Physics is principles and mathematics; chemistry is all this learning, and biology is even more learning. And that was it. That sort of got me going.

**[00:08:09] Michael Levitt:** There were other lucky circumstances. At the end of my BSc degree, I had done well, and I wanted to do a PhD with Kendrew, who had given this program. I wrote to him and he said no. But then I was again very lucky because my best friends at university were business school people, and they said, 'You know, in business, if somebody says no, you don't just say okay. You come back with a counteroffer.'

**[00:08:31] Michael Levitt:** So my counteroffer was: 'If I can't come this year, I can come next year.' A pretty trivial counteroffer, but it changed everything. In science, they had never had a counteroffer like that. So they said, 'Well, why don't you come and see us?' I went to see them and I met all these really great scientists—people like Francis Crick and Max Perutz, all these Nobel laureates who were giants in the field, because Cambridge had really invented molecular biology.

**[00:08:54] Michael Levitt:** After seeing me, they said, 'Well, we enjoyed you. We'll tell you in a year's time whether we'll accept you,' which means I now had a gap year. Then again, my business school friends said, 'Come on, you haven't gained anything; you still don't know what you're going to do with your year.' So—something that was very difficult for me—I got the courage to drive up to Cambridge and announced myself without an appointment, hanging around in the corridor. I happened to see Max Perutz, who was the leader of the place. An amazing man who had a Nobel Prize, but his real claim to fame is he was the best social engineer I have ever met. He had empathy that was amazing.

**[00:09:37] Michael Levitt:** I caught him in the corridor, shaking—I was always scared to talk to him—saying, 'Look, my finals are coming up. The uncertainty of my life is worrying me. Can we talk?' We went into the room, and he basically said, 'Look, I'm the co-leader of this place with John Kendrew. John isn't here today; I'll let you know on Monday.' Then I had the good sense just to say, 'Well, thank you very much,' and beat a retreat. By Monday, I had been accepted, but on one condition: John Kendrew said he wouldn't take me that year; I had to go to Israel for a year.

**[00:10:14] Michael Levitt:** I never really understood why somebody who was not Jewish would send me to Israel, until I discovered that during the Second World War he was in the RAF and had known Chaim Weizmann's son, the president of Israel's son, who was a pilot and was killed in a plane accident. He then met other Israelis and actually wanted to visit Israel. He didn't do that, but instead he was on the scientific advisory board of the Weizmann Institute, and he knew that there was somebody there who would be very important for me.

**[00:10:49] Michael Levitt:** Again, all of this was unknown to me. I didn't want to go there. I said, 'This is backward, who wants to go to Israel? I want to go to Tokyo, I want to go to Boston!' This was in '67, right after the Six-Day War. But he said, 'Look, you're going to go to Israel.' Not only that, he basically bribed me to go to Israel: the Royal Society had just set up an exchange program with the Israeli Academy to exchange postdoctoral fellows, and he said, 'Look, this year there was a war in Israel, we have no candidates. I will get you a postdoctoral fellowship.' Remember, I had just finished my BSc degree—I was not a post-doc! But they got me a postdoctoral fellowship to go to Israel, and this was like three times more money than I'd ever had in a year. I went, and there I met the people who essentially resulted in the Nobel Prize.

**[00:11:43] Michael Levitt:** So basically, there's a very tight connection between playing snooker as a child and getting the Nobel Prize, which you would never have predicted!

**[00:11:51] Gita Wirjawan:** You were coexisting with quite a number of Nobel laureates. Did you ever think that you would someday win?

**[00:11:58] Michael Levitt:** Not really. I didn't at that time in the '60s. I didn't even expect it 40 years later. I was working in computation starting in 1970, and really even before '67, so I was really pioneering what later became computational biology. But computers in biology were not seen as a natural fit, although if you think about it, biology is complicated and computers are great for complexity.

**[00:12:27] Michael Levitt:** Maybe in a different time I would have been a hacker breaking into bank accounts and things like that—instead, no, I'm too old for that! But instead, I really loved computers. Computers were actually very important for crystallography. What I did besides molecular dynamics was actually do a lot of things in crystallography.

**[00:12:52] Michael Levitt:** I was very lucky because when I went to Cambridge a year later, I really felt that I had my PhD in the bag—I already had a few interesting papers. So I was able to look around and do other things. I was like a freelancing first-year PhD student. I worked with Francis Crick, Aaron Klug, and Max Perutz—I have papers with all of them. It was an amazing situation. They were not in computing—there was almost nobody in computing—but they were starting to see its value.

**[00:13:23] Michael Levitt:** For example, DNA sequencing became a big deal. I was actually asked to work on the very first bit of nucleic acid that had been sequenced from a phage. When the person came to me, I said, 'No, no, I'm not really interested in DNA; I'm going to keep working on proteins.' That was back then! So I was really lucky because Cambridge was absolute Ground Zero for this field. In the end, from this one laboratory of about 300 people, they ended up getting 28 Nobel Prizes among people who either worked there or got their Nobel Prize-winning idea there. I knew these people, and I was very, very lucky.

**[00:14:07] Michael Levitt:** Another bit of luck: when I was in Israel for that one year, I actually got married to my wife Reena (she passed away 50 years later, but we had a long marriage). Her best friend, who I also knew in Israel, was a young lady named Shosh (Shulamit) who was hitchhiking in Israel in 1968. By chance, Francis Crick had come to Israel to visit, and he had already won the Nobel Prize. He was super famous, traveling with a friend, and they picked her up while hitchhiking. This woman decided to go with them and stayed with them for their tour. At the end, she said, 'You know, my best friend has just moved to Cambridge. Would you give her these books?' Now I'm in Cambridge, a beginning PhD student, and suddenly I get a call from Francis Crick saying, 'I have books for you, why don't you come over?' So these relationships and serendipity were enormously important.

**[00:15:20] Michael Levitt:** Now that I'm part of the Nobel group, I've met many Nobel laureates, and there's even another one in whose life snooker played a big role—a man named Rich Roberts. All these stories talk about serendipity, and they all talk about how often they failed. Failure is a very important part of success.

**[00:15:52] Gita Wirjawan:** It's quite amazing how you could be so divergent, moving from one dimension to another. You studied physics, tinkered with biology, won the Nobel in chemistry, played snooker...

**[00:16:08] Michael Levitt:** And I'm now very interested in epidemiology! I think they actually are connected. I believe that disciplines are artificial. One reason why we have disciplines is very simple: if you're in chemistry at Stanford and want to hire junior faculty, you want them to be a great researcher, but you also want them to teach Chemistry 101. You want somebody to teach Chemistry 101 who took Chemistry 101! Very few physicists would have taken Chemistry 101, so you end up reinforcing the silos.

**[00:16:45] Michael Levitt:** Stanford actually made a huge effort to dismantle the silos with the Bio-X program. 'X' means 'Bio-anything'—an unknown in an equation, not just biophysics or biochemistry, but Bio-computing, Bio-whatever. This program has been really important at breaking down silos. In Israel, people are in the same building—there aren't departmental offices, but people from 30 different faculties are in the same building. This has now been replicated in engineering centers and elsewhere. Silos that existed for teaching reasons are breaking down. In some ways, the best person to teach Chemistry 101 is an online recording of the best teacher ever.

**[00:18:03] Gita Wirjawan:** You came up with the term 'molecular dynamics'. You were critical at some point about how it was unable to refine proteins. Is there hope that that's going to happen?

**[00:18:20] Michael Levitt:** Molecular dynamics is how proteins move. Static structures of proteins look like sculptures, but proteins are in continual movement—a consequence of room temperature. If proteins weren't moving, we would be dead. Warm-blooded creatures evolved to optimize the way proteins work. A moving protein is a real protein. My skepticism came from the fact that a lot of this movement is just incidental movement, but it's been very valuable to look at moving models rather than static ones. In computers, we can make movies of moving models, and experimentalists can see pieces coming together that they didn't realize could interact.

**[00:19:35] Michael Levitt:** Computers now have a massive role in biology. What we were recognized for was the first real use of computers in biology. You mentioned the Volvo metaphor—let me explain that.

**[00:20:00] Michael Levitt:** There were three people involved in my Nobel Prize: Martin Karplus (a senior investigator from Harvard), Arieh Warshel (whose programmer I had been when I went to Israel in 1967), and me. Arieh had been a post-doc with Karplus but didn't feel Karplus had furthered his career properly. When we won, Arieh went to the organizers and said he didn't want Karplus to give the banquet speech. So it fell upon me to give the 2-minute speech in front of 1,200 people, including the King of Sweden.

**[00:21:19] Michael Levitt:** I decided to start my speech in Swedish, even though I don't speak Swedish. I had a Swedish post-doc translate my opening line: 'Your Majesty, Your Excellencies, ladies and gentlemen, I start my talk in Swedish to show that I can still learn something 45 years after finishing the work that brought me here.' Then I repeated it in English, and the King was smiling like crazy!

**[00:22:05] Michael Levitt:** I then joked about Sweden's carbonated vodka, and went on to tell the Volvo story. The San Jose Mercury News had asked me how much computers had changed. Instead of just giving a boring answer about Moore's Law, I calculated that computers had improved dramatically in four areas: they became smaller, faster, cheaper, and had greater capacity.

**[00:23:52] Michael Levitt:** I said that if motorcars had progressed the same way over those 45 years, we would see a Volvo costing $1, carrying 50,000 people in comfort, driving at 2 million kilometers per hour, and parking inside a shoebox! Each of those factors had improved by about 10,000 times. Everything is interesting if you retain the curiosity of a 5-year-old.

**[00:25:21] Gita Wirjawan:** Extending on this, how do you see the role of AI in simulating what you've been doing?

**[00:25:27] Michael Levitt:** Everything I've been doing has been AI of one kind or another—machine learning, least squares. We've seen a dramatic change from punch cards and 6-hour wait times to an iPhone more powerful than all the world's computers 50 years ago.

**[00:25:55] Michael Levitt:** Large language models were an incredible breakthrough for me. I vividly remember my first encounter with ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) on a beach in Israel around December 1, 2022. My wife Shoshana is a curator of Chinese art, and she was scheduled to have an exhibition for a Chinese artist 3 weeks later. Her friend's husband told us about ChatGPT. Shoshana typed in a prompt asking for a curatorial speech for the exhibition, and out came a great speech! We asked it again, and out came another different, great speech!

**[00:27:35] Michael Levitt:** I got hooked immediately. I now have over 10,000 sessions with ChatGPT. Initially, people were scared about existential threats, but nuclear war and human stupidity are much greater existential threats. AI has the potential to make us all smarter.

**[00:28:34] Michael Levitt:** Initially, in a narcissistic way, I asked GPT about my role during COVID, and it wrote nice, unbiased summaries. Then I started using it for programming and writing abstracts. On a train from Shanghai to Beijing, we uploaded photographs of an artist's lockdown artwork—photographs of trees and rocks hanging from cranes—and ChatGPT immediately recognized the themes of dislocation, isolation, andNet roots! It was incredible. It's like having a smart, dispassionate friend.

**[00:32:45] Gita Wirjawan:** How about those who are not as smart as you? How will AI impact them?

**[00:32:51] Michael Levitt:** It's no more wrong than social media or search engines. The average person is way smarter than leaders make them out to be. Leaders sometimes harness resentment, but everyday people adapt quickly. Smartphones with AI are massive democratizing tools.

**[00:34:04] Gita Wirjawan:** Do you get the sense that AI is being pushed forth in an adequately multidisciplinary manner?

**[00:34:17] Michael Levitt:** I think it is. Fake news and propaganda have been around for centuries—Gutenberg's printing press was heavily used for fake news, and during Isaac Newton's era (1650–1720), false posters and wrongful arrests were rampant. Europe has always had periods of instability, which actually drive innovation.

**[00:36:38] Gita Wirjawan:** Does stability stifle creativity?

**[00:36:38] Michael Levitt:** China's emphasis on stability can stifle creativity. The real question is about governance. Nobody has a monopoly on the right governance scheme. I treat life as a buffet: take what you like and don't complain about what you don't like. Every system has good and bad sides. Uncontrolled capitalism leads to inequality, which is fraying the US system, but diversity and innovation remain incredible strengths.

**[00:38:48] Gita Wirjawan:** You saw the internet as the democratizer of information, but it didn't lead to a commensurate democratization of ideas and capital.

**[00:39:03] Michael Levitt:** Capital is controlled by those who have it, but the number of people above the poverty line globally has increased tremendously. Child mortality has dropped. Smartphones are the ultimate enablers—even refugees on Greek islands rely on smartphones for their memories, communication, and education. My 12 grandchildren use the internet to learn complex history through animated videos.

**[00:42:31] Gita Wirjawan:** Put this in the context of biological evolution. We started out as bacteria about a billion years ago—where are we going?

**[00:42:47] Michael Levitt:** There are three sources of intelligence on Earth: Human Intelligence (HI), Artificial Intelligence (AI), and Biological Intelligence (BI). Biological Intelligence is the big one. It invented molecules, self-assembly, and evolution. A protein is a machine 5 nanometers thick that assembles itself. Everything humans manufacture is made from the outside in a factory, but biology builds from the inside out.

**[00:44:15] Michael Levitt:** Neural networks in AI are based on a 1943 model of a nerve cell. So AI is directly informed by biology. The key lesson of biological evolution is diversity.

**[00:45:07] Michael Levitt:** Bacteria reproduce by cloning—one cell copies itself. They survived, but didn't create macroscopic diversity. Then about a billion years ago, eukaryotic cells invented sexual reproduction—combining genes from a mother and a father. Nature didn't select a system to give only the 'best' genes; it gave a random 50% from each parent. Why? For the exact same reason a good banker diversifies: we don't know the future!

**[00:47:41] Michael Levitt:** The long-term survival of a species depends on diversity. Human behavior and cultural diversity are extensions of this. Equality and caring for every individual—including the vulnerable—is a biological necessity for survival because we don't know which genes or perspectives will be needed in the future.

**[00:51:07] Michael Levitt:** At Stanford, programs like Bio-X and seed grants ($20,000–$50,000 in unrestricted funds) encourage random pairings of scientists from different disciplines. It's like mother and father coming together to create a brand-new interdisciplinary field.

**[00:55:47] Michael Levitt:** I studied NIH grant distribution and Nobel laureate chains. The median age of first-time NIH grantees rose by 20 years over a 30-year period because older evaluators lose connection with 20-year-olds! Young people (20-year-olds) are special because they don't know too much—Bill Gates, Steve Jobs, Mark Zuckerberg, Larry Page, Sergey Brin all started in their 20s. Senior experts know too much to be truly disruptive.

**[00:57:59] Gita Wirjawan:** What about nations that are highly homogeneous in terms of their long-term survival?

**[00:58:07] Michael Levitt:** They will become more diverse through globalization, digital media, and AI. AI and robotics will also solve demographic aging challenges. For instance, Japan, South Korea, and Northern Italy have rapidly aging populations; home care robots will handle elderly care within 5 years, reducing the friction around immigration.

**[00:10:06] Michael Levitt:** AI will also act as an emotional and strategic advisor. Look at Meta's 'Cicero' AI, which became a world champion at the negotiation game 'Diplomacy'. Human players reported that Cicero was incredibly polite, never backstabbed anyone, and won purely through fair, clever strategy.

**[00:11:07] Michael Levitt:** Simultaneous translation on smartphones in 5 years will eliminate language barriers entirely, allowing people to communicate across cultures seamlessly.

**[00:13:32] Gita Wirjawan:** I want to touch on COVID-19. You came out in 2020...

**[00:13:41] Michael Levitt:** In late January 2020, I looked at the COVID numbers in China. As a mathematician, I tested for exponential growth by calculating the ratio of daily deaths. The ratio was actually dropping steadily along a straight line, showing that the outbreak would peak quickly. I wrote a report on February 1, 2020, which was translated into Chinese and went viral with millions of views.

**[00:18:17] Michael Levitt:** Data from the Diamond Princess cruise ship showed a mortality rate of 0.004% (7 deaths out of 1,400 passengers, mostly elderly). Data from Wuhan showed COVID almost exclusively targeted the elderly and sick. Globally, about 60 million people die naturally every year (180 million over 3 years). The ~7 million COVID deaths represented about 7 weeks of extra mortality over 3 years—roughly a 3.5% bump.

**[00:12:31] Michael Levitt:** When economists analyze Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALY), COVID deaths strongly matched natural age-mortality profiles. Furthermore, our study published in PNAS showed that excess mortality across nations correlated most strongly with pre-existing socioeconomic factors: low GDP, high Gini inequality, and high poverty—not strictness of lockdowns.

**[00:13:25] Gita Wirjawan:** How would you compare mRNA vaccines with non-mRNA vaccines?

**[00:13:30] Michael Levitt:** mRNA vaccines inject genetic instructions wrapped in lipid nanoparticles, allowing any cell type to produce the spike protein. Traditional vaccines (like Sinovac's inactivated virus) introduce intact protein antigens. Because mRNA nanoparticles can enter non-target cells, T-cells may kill those host cells, potentially causing localized side effects in endothelium or heart tissue.

**[00:14:49] Gita Wirjawan:** Is the coronavirus going to mutate into something much worse in the future?

**[00:14:57] Michael Levitt:** COVID is like the Pope; Influenza is like the head of the Mafia! Influenza has a segmented RNA genome (8 pieces) that mutates violently and lacks error-checking. Coronaviruses have a single long RNA genome (32,000 bases) with proofreading mechanisms. COVID is far less dangerous in terms of chaotic mutation than flu.

**[00:15:36] Michael Levitt:** Our immune systems are remarkably smart. To stay healthy, focus on nutrition, exercise, sleep, happiness, and don't over-sterilize your hands!

**[00:19:04] Gita Wirjawan:** What advice do you have for the young in order for them to be as curious as you and become good scientists?

**[00:19:25] Michael Levitt:** First, retain the curiosity of a 5-year-old. Second, be open and take chances. Third, do something you love. Fourth, realize that young people are precious—we need you to break old systems and innovate!

**[00:20:43] Michael Levitt:** Finally, learn to make mistakes! A good scientist is wrong 90% of the time, and a really good scientist is wrong 99% of the time because they ask harder questions. If you are afraid of making mistakes, you cannot innovate. Use AI as your personal tutor to experiment, fail, learn, and grow!

**[00:21:34] Gita Wirjawan:** Professor Michael Levitt, thank you so much for your time.

**[00:21:40] Michael Levitt:** Thank you so much!
