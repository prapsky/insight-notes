# Patient Capital (*Uang Sabar*)

[[entities/indrawan-nugroho|Indrawan Nugroho]] argues that long-term innovation needs **patient money**. Examples include Amazon-style reinvestment, large climate projects, and difficult products that take years to build. Patient capital does not have to be returned and reinvested within only a few years.

Indonesia’s main constraint is often **impatient capital** combined with a financial system that has little money circulating through it. The problem is not only a shortage of ambitious founders. Source: [[sources/012-endgame-65-indrawan-nugroho|Endgame #65]].

## Two kinds of capital

| | Patient (*uang sabar*) | Impatient |
|--|------------------------|-----------|
| Typical example | US pension funds and other long-duration pools of about 20–30 years | Local funds that recycle in about 7 years; middleman-style funding measured in months |
| Founder behavior | Reinvest, delay an exit, and measure long-term progress | Focus on daily revenue and accept an early exit |
| Policy message | Encouragement alone is not enough | Founders are told to “think long-term” while their money must return quickly |

In Indrawan’s framing, Amazon’s cycle of innovation, customer focus, and lower costs depended on powerful chief executives *and* investors who did not demand the rapid return of their original capital.

## Money supply compared with GDP

Indrawan cites circulating money equal to about **45% of GDP** in Indonesia. He compares this with about **150–250%** in advanced economies and about **125%** in Singapore. Raising this ratio with money that is also patient could make room for billion-dollar startups outside the usual centers.

This tells a scarcity story similar in scale to [[concepts/money-supply-vs-interest-rates]]. That page covers Purbaya’s M0 money growth, Chatib’s limits from savings and the Incremental Capital Output Ratio, and the scarcity narratives of Gita and Martyn. Indrawan’s prescription differs. He emphasizes the *duration* of capital and founder culture, not only the total stock of money.

## Bootstrap when capital won’t wait

Indrawan tells a story about an Indian digital company serving micro, small, and medium enterprises. It rejected a large venture-capital investment with a **7-year exit** clause. The company funded itself, delayed salaries until it reached a revenue target, and stayed independent.

This is not a model for every company. It shows that mission and patience can sometimes substitute for outside capital when an investor’s timeline conflicts with the product’s timeline.

## Founder patience = capital patience

Demographic change may fill the world with capital chasing a limited supply of traditional assets. But patient funds will not help if *operators* still optimize for daily revenue or quick cryptocurrency gains.

Education should teach micro, small, and medium enterprises and startups to measure long-term progress, such as active users, and to delay personal profit. Investor timelines must change too. This complements [[concepts/deep-tech-long-termism]], where Martyn argues that intellectual property can take a decade to build and that money is not a complete solution. Patient capital, however, is not limited to deep technology.
