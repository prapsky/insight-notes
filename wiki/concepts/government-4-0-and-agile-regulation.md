# Government 4.0 and Agile Regulation

[[entities/chatib-basri|Chatib Basri]] argues in [[sources/006-endgame-216-chatib-basri|Endgame #216]] that developing countries should worry less about catching up with **Industry 4.0** and more about building **Government 4.0**. Technology can make government rules obsolete within months, while laws cannot be changed at the same speed.

## Diagnosis

- Communication moved from postcards to WhatsApp and file transfer from USB drives to AirDrop. Regulators still write rules for the previous technology.
- Detailed instructions about *how* to do something become outdated quickly, while laws are difficult to amend.
- “Agile bureaucracy is an **oxymoron**,” meaning the two ideas conflict. Government should not pretend it can move as quickly as a startup.

## Prescription: principles over prescriptions

Move from **agreeing on detailed rules** to **agreeing on broad principles**:

- For example, agree on financial inclusion, no fraud, and consumer protection. Let markets and regulators choose the methods.
- Governments should not turn procedures designed for the era of USB drives into permanent law.

This goes with private-sector **retraining**. A double tax deduction can encourage companies to train workers. Government training centers, known as BLK, often use outdated tools. Industrial policy can therefore use incentives instead of outdated public workshops.

## Link to AI / capital

AI is **engineering that requires large amounts of capital**, including scale and data centers. It is not “just software.” Leadership is concentrated in very large US and Chinese firms. Staying outside their capital and talent networks can worsen the [[concepts/middle-class-squeeze|concentration of benefits among elites]]. Slow regulation makes the gap larger.

## Bravo 500 panel (#007): same prescription, infra stakes

[[entities/budi-gunadi-sadikin|Budi]] says competitive technology sectors need light-touch regulation based on principles. Monopolies can remain governed by detailed rules, but fixed rules in competitive sectors cannot keep pace with technology. [[entities/sai-prasad|Sai]] says products improve exponentially while regulators tend to learn at a linear pace. He therefore supports regulatory **sandboxes**, such as those used by Singapore’s government technology sector, and better digital literacy among officials. Governments should start with technology that exists today and then add safeguards, much as a developer first writes a “hello world” program and later builds a protective boundary around it. This argument connects to [[concepts/physical-vs-cryptographic-sovereignty]] because adoption should not stop while the country waits for perfect laws governing very large local data centers. The rejection of **power wheeling**, which would let private generators send electricity through the public grid, is a concrete missed deregulation opportunity under [[concepts/ai-electrification-threshold]].
