# Scientific Serendipity and Failure

[[entities/michael-levitt|Michael Levitt]] says scientific careers often grow through chains of **unintended consequences**. A Nobel Prize cannot be planned and depends partly on unpredictable luck. He also argues that **failure is a necessary part of success**. Source: [[sources/004-endgame-193-michael-levitt|Endgame #193]].

## The pattern in his career

1. A night playing snooker led to his mother challenging him to “skip school.” This led to early university entry.
2. Watching *Thread of Life* on London television sparked his interest in molecular biology.
3. Kendrew said **no**, but a business school made a **counteroffer**. This led to a Cambridge interview.
4. He resisted spending a required year at Weizmann. That year led to his collaboration with Warshel and, eventually, the Nobel Prize.
5. A friend he met while hitchhiking, plus books by Crick, gave him early access to an important scientific network.

Levitt jokes that there is a “very tight connection between playing snooker as a child and getting the Nobel Prize.”

## Advice for young people

- Keep the curiosity of a five-year-old.
- Stay open, take chances, and do what you love.
- Young people are valuable disruptors because senior people can “know too much.” In his telling, the age at which researchers received their first NIH grant rose by about 20 years over roughly 30 years.
- Scientists may be **wrong 90% of the time**, while great scientists may be **wrong 99% of the time** because they ask harder questions. Fear of mistakes kills innovation.
- Use AI as a personal tutor. Experiment, fail, and learn.

## Bridge in this vault

This idea pairs with Tom Lembong’s [[concepts/risk-as-innovation-fuel|risk as innovation fuel]]. Entrepreneurs need to be able to fail without criminal stigma. Scientists need to treat failure as the normal path toward difficult questions. The fields differ, but both need a culture in which productive failure is survivable.
