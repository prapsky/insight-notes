# Middle-Class Squeeze

[[entities/chatib-basri|Chatib Basri]] argues in [[sources/006-endgame-216-chatib-basri|Endgame #216]] that many governments, including Indonesia, protect the **poor** through social programs and allow the **rich** to capture gains from growth. But they have **no policy designed specifically for the middle class**. He connects this to Sebastian Edwards’s “Chile paradox,” which he discusses after hearing Michelle Bachelet at a Harvard forum for ministers.

## Indonesian pattern he cites (2019–2023)

| Group | Income growth (his telling) | Policy experience |
|-------|----------------------------|-------------------|
| Bottom ~40% (deciles 0–4) | Still positive, slowing | BLT, PKH, social protection |
| Middle ~deciles 5–8 | **Negative** | Mostly outside personal income tax; tax breaks have little effect |
| Top ~20% | ~+4% | Enjoy growth / high-end consumption (e.g. iPhone concerts) |

In his data series, the middle class grew from about 5% of the population in 2003 to about 18% in 2014. It peaked at about 21–23%, then fell to about 17% in 2023. Definitions are contested, so he recommends examining each income tenth rather than relying on averages.

This resembles Branko Milanovic’s **elephant curve**: income growth dips in the middle and rises at the top. It helps explain why middle-class purchasing power can weaken while demand for luxury goods remains strong.

## Why tax relief fails this band

He describes the squeezed middle as households spending about Rp800,000 to Rp9.9 million a month. Most are **below the personal income-tax threshold**, so personal income-tax holidays do not help. They need **targeted tools outside income tax**. One example he supported was an electricity discount for households using 2,200 watts or less when value-added tax rose.

## Broader inequality stack

He pairs the squeeze with:

- Inequality of opportunity, wealth, and income
- **Centripetal** development, meaning growth concentrates in major cities instead of spreading to secondary cities and regions.
- Internet and AI access, together with limits on bank credit, can keep capital in elite hands despite claims of “financial inclusion.”

Long-run antidote he names first: education and training — see [[concepts/stem-talent-pipeline]], [[concepts/soft-infrastructure-teachers]]. Political risk: inequality feeds rightward, fear-based populism (closing arc of #216).
