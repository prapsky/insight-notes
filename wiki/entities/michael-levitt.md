# Michael Levitt

Michael Levitt is a professor of structural biology at Stanford Medical School. He is also associated with computer science. In 2013, he shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Martin Karplus and Arieh Warshel. Their computational methods made it practical to model chemical reactions and the movement of proteins. He appears in this wiki through [[sources/004-endgame-193-michael-levitt|Endgame #193]], an interview with [[entities/gita-wirjawan|Gita Wirjawan]].

## Why he appears here

Levitt is the main source for several ideas:

- [[concepts/biological-intelligence|Biological intelligence]] compares biological, human, and artificial intelligence. It also looks at how biology builds from the inside out.
- [[concepts/diversity-as-evolutionary-strategy|Diversity as evolutionary strategy]] explains diversity as protection against an unknown future. It treats sexual reproduction like a portfolio of different possibilities.
- [[concepts/interdisciplinary-silos|Interdisciplinary silos]] argues that subject boundaries often come from how universities teach. Bio-X is one attempt to cross those boundaries.
- [[concepts/scientific-serendipity-and-failure|Scientific serendipity & failure]] shows how luck, counteroffers, and being wrong 90–99% of the time can still lead to discovery.

Levitt also argues that AI can be a tutor and make knowledge more widely available. He uses ChatGPT heavily. He ranks nuclear war and human stupidity above the risk that AI could end humanity. His COVID and epidemiology claims from the same interview are kept limited and contested on the source page. They are not promoted as established claims here.

## Career arc (from Endgame #193)

Levitt grew up in Pretoria, South Africa, and pushed to finish school early. A London television program, *Thread of Life*, introduced him to John Kendrew's work. He studied physics at King's College. He then spent a year at the Weizmann Institute, where he worked with Warshel and began the path that later led to the Nobel Prize. He entered molecular biology at Cambridge when the field was taking shape. He joined Stanford in 1987. At the Nobel banquet, he used a “Volvo” metaphor to explain computing. In later years, he became enthusiastic about large language models.
