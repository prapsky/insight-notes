## Summary

Keyu begins with her education in China, at Horace Mann, and at Harvard. She then explains China’s political economy. Politics is centralized, but cities compete to attract businesses and produce growth. She calls this the [[concepts/mayor-economy|mayor economy]]. She argues that Western economic models often overlook state capacity: a government’s ability to coordinate and carry out policy. The same Chinese system can produce technology unicorns, but also debt, excess industrial capacity, and weak household spending. Other themes include treating US–China relations as competition rather than a clash of civilizations; improving and spreading inventions, including during crises caused by export controls; the self-harm caused by protectionism and financial weapons; lessons for Southeast Asia; China as a provider of affordable technology and capital; productivity as more important than population size; and a multipolar “coalition of the willing.”

## Core claims (attributed)

### Formation

1. **She moved to the United States alone at age 14.** She was the only international student at Horace Mann in New York. Her parents were supportive but apprehensive, while China was then in an “accelerator mode” of seizing new opportunities.
2. **Her US education taught her to question authority and keep an open mind.** Her history teacher, Mr. DeVito, taught historiography, bias, and the absence of one universal truth for an exam. This differed from Chinese textbooks focused on dates and facts. Chinese education had given her strong academic tools, and she ranked first academically. US education unlocked assertion and interpretation. She sees unique value in combining both systems.
3. **She chose economics because it offers both breadth and depth.** In Gita’s framing, her father was a Shakespeare scholar and her mother was a concert pianist. Economics gave Keyu mathematical tools while letting her study history, society, psychology, and political economy. Its subjects range from the wages of sumo wrestlers to the one-child policy and monetary policy in an open economy.

### China model (spine)

4. **Adaptiveness is not the same as Western open-mindedness.** After Deng’s opening, China broke with anti-capitalist ideology and became agile and pragmatic while remaining conservative in its values. As a fertility example, policy rapidly shifted toward encouraging single women to have children when demographic conditions demanded it.
5. **Western textbooks give too little weight to state capacity.** Standard models often limit government to correcting market failures and managing the overall economy. China’s experience adds mobilization, coordination, resource allocation, and strategic direction. This is an **entrepreneurial state**. It can strongly shape development, but it is not always right.
6. **The [[concepts/mayor-economy|mayor economy]] is a growth tournament among local governments.** Firms can leave one city for a rival, and even districts within Beijing hold “beauty contests” to attract startups. The same logic now drives technology. Local and central funds support semiconductors and digital industries, while civil servants are becoming more technically literate. For example, the mayor of Shanghai was previously president of Tsinghua University. The system still depends on private enterprises.
7. **Gita identifies a decentralization paradox.** China combines centralized politics with decentralized economic competition. Many democracies, especially in Southeast Asia, combine decentralized politics with centralized economics. Keyu says Singapore is only partly similar because a small city-state lacks the **competition among localities** that disciplines Chinese mayors.
8. **Decentralized democracies can reward successful local reforms.** They can combine accountability with public recognition for local leaders and copy reforms that work. Keyu argues that well-used state capacity can be effective, noting that Western governments are now adopting industrial policy. The difficult historical point is that periods of fastest growth have not always been democratic, and Western cultures of extended debate can move more slowly than China’s early regulation of technology.
9. **China’s system contains some democratic values without being a democracy.** Keyu points to merit-based recruitment, responsiveness to complaints, and concern about misuse of data. She also warns that growing populism and reluctance to create losers have made difficult economic decisions harder than they were 10–15 years ago. In her account, ordinary citizens focus more on schools, housing, and marriage than on asking when democracy will arrive.

### US–China rivalry (secondary)

10. **The United States and China are not opposites.** Both are competitive, enjoy winning, value family, reward merit, and have high social mobility. Keyu considers them more similar to each other than China is to many other cultures. Their relationship works best when scientists and businesses cooperate.
11. **Their clash combines rivalry with miscommunication.** The United States is not used to having a competitor across the full range of technology, while China communicates its intentions poorly. Saying that China will be “number one in everything” sounds almost harmless within China but frightening abroad.
12. **Keyu says China does not aim to displace the United States soon.** China may surpass the United States in total gross domestic product, but catching up in output per person remains far away. Producing cutting-edge technology at about 25% of Western living standards is historically unusual and, in her view, justifies pride. She attributes this result to a hybrid state and strength in people, engineering, and education.
13. **Competition between major countries can benefit the world.** It prevents one country from holding monopolies, while China’s emphasis on practical solutions and lower costs can serve the adoption needs of developing economies. ([[concepts/process-and-diffusion-innovation]])
14. **Trying to reindustrialize through tariffs can misfire.** A falling manufacturing share is part of a development cycle that moved from the United Kingdom to Germany, Japan, the United States, and eventually China. Building security-related capacity is not the same as bringing back basic manufacturing. Technology, rather than trade, drives most changes in inequality and skill gaps. China’s combination of **scale, production, and cutting-edge technology** is difficult to reproduce, though India may do so later. Keyu says Tesla’s Shanghai factory saved Tesla, while moving TSMC production to the United States has been slow.
15. **Keyu rejects the portrayal of the United States as a victim of the liberal economic order.** The United States dominates services exports and possesses unmatched financial leverage through the dollar and the SWIFT system. The Federal Reserve can optimize policy for the United States while the rest of the world adjusts. In her view, trying to restore basic manufacturing through protectionism would create a self-inflicted wound.
16. **Using [[concepts/weaponized-interdependence-and-substitutes|economic leverage encourages substitutes]].** Historical examples include Napoleon’s continental system, Britain’s competition with Germany and the United States, and the United States’ competition with Japan. Creating short-term pain does not guarantee long-term dominance. Using the dollar as a weapon and restricting Chinese rare-earth exports both encourage alternatives. When entangled economies use leverage against each other, their connections can unravel faster. No dominant power remains dominant forever, and knowledge now spreads more quickly because AI requires less accumulated physical infrastructure than earlier technologies.
17. **A shift toward “jungle rules” damages US credibility.** It alienates allies and can gradually erode the country’s role as a peacemaker and guardian of trade. Keyu considers a zero-sum national-security lens dangerous, especially when developing countries often see growth as positive-sum.
18. **People-to-people exchange is sharply imbalanced.** Gita cites roughly 250,000–350,000 Chinese students in the United States but only about 700 Americans in China. American exceptionalism and a dominant media narrative contribute to the gap, while China also makes itself unattractive through barriers such as visas. Keyu says Americans who have visited China rarely remain entirely negative. She proposes managing rivalry through small wins that provide public goods, including environmental cooperation, fentanyl control, green development, and transparent data. Crises can also force central banks to cooperate.
19. **Formal respect and reciprocity matter in negotiations with China.** Keyu argues that the Chinese side places great importance on visible respect and that US negotiators often underestimate this need, producing worse agreements.

### Innovation (secondary)

20. **[[concepts/process-and-diffusion-innovation|Improving and spreading technology can matter more than inventing it first]].** Bezos treats lower-cost processes as innovation. There are few truly general-purpose breakthroughs, such as electricity and AI. Spreading them through the economy can matter more than being first. China is strong at solving practical problems, reverse engineering, and finding creative alternatives rather than making “another app.”
21. **[[concepts/crisis-innovation-and-tech-restrictions|Export controls can trigger crisis innovation]].** Keyu says the Trump administration’s 2018 controls caused China, for the first time, to make science and technology a leading national priority. She presents DeepSeek as the beginning of a larger wave supported by central and local funds, firms, and universities. This mobilization continues despite a weak overall economy, falling private investment, and geopolitical shocks, creating a contrast between macroeconomic weakness and a dynamic technology sector.

### Global South / SEA / AI (secondary)

22. **Splitting the world economy into two systems would raise inflation.** During China’s World Trade Organization era, it exported lower prices. Restricting those flows instead exports inflation through intermediate goods, and China accounts for about 30% of manufactured goods in this framing. Rerouting supply chains is costly. Goods described as replacements from Mexico, Canada, or the European Union often still contain Chinese inputs. In developing countries, more of a tariff tends to pass through into inflation.
23. **Southeast Asia can learn from several East Asian economies, not only the mayor economy.** Korea, Japan, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Singapore combined high savings, Confucian education and human capital, macroeconomic and political stability, and industrialization. High savings helped during their growth stages, though Keyu says excessive savings are now a problem for China. Countries should avoid premature deindustrialization and the commodity curse. They need their own investment, can use digital systems to leap ahead in permits, and should give people hope that their children will do better. The trade war represents a **restructuring of globalization, not deglobalization**. Chinese foreign direct investment in Southeast Asia and Mexico is therefore a major opportunity for third countries.
24. **China can allocate technology capital to developing economies.** Gita contrasts affordable, capable products such as OPPO phones with iPhones and argues that Western financial capital is blocked when uncertainty cannot be priced. ([[concepts/uncertainty-to-risk-translation]]) Keyu presents China’s approach as positive-sum: prosperity can create more prosperity, and China can provide technology and productive capacity to the Global South, including in the energy race.
25. **Demographics are not China’s largest challenge.** Productivity matters more than the number of workers, as shown by comparing output per US worker with output per Indian worker. It is contradictory to fear both youth unemployment from AI and automation and a labor shortage caused by aging. Keyu also says aging is not the source of the US fiscal problem, and that aging rich countries adopted technology faster. China should train workers for a technology shock. A 1% reduction in its still-large labor force is not critical.
26. **AI could increase inequality between countries.** Globalization increased inequality within many countries while reducing it between countries, but unequal access to AI may reverse the latter trend. Coordinated access could instead help poorer countries leap ahead. Keyu calls for AI governance comparable to Bretton Woods so development needs remain represented. She contrasts China’s language of dignity and livelihoods with the United States’ moral emphasis on liberal democracy.
27. **The energy race could make advanced technology more exclusive.** Keyu agrees that a competition for energy is coming, but she expects ingenuity to create alternatives, as it did in response to OPEC. Developing countries will need support, and she is hopeful that China can play a constructive role.
28. **Most countries prefer a multipolar and multilateral order.** Keyu says one leading country is the main exception. She cites the Chinese premier’s unilateral tariff cuts for least-developed countries and argues that middle powers such as Indonesia can thrive in a multipolar system. Asia is especially suited to regionalization. Cultural and political differences should remain separate from pragmatic economic goals, as shown by continuing semiconductor cooperation among Korea, Japan, and China.
29. **A practical accommodation between the United States and China is realistic.** The stakes are too high for a major eruption, so Keyu expects managed fluctuations in the relationship. She believes the United States will eventually accept China as a close competitor and focus more on domestic issues. Attempts at suppression are unlikely to work because knowledge spreads.

## Notable quotes / framings

- “I don’t really think that US and China are as different as they tend to be or claim to be.”
- On Fed/dollar: “Whatever it decides to do, the rest of the world will have to go along with it.”
- “I don’t know why the US administration pretends to be a victim… the US has hugely benefited from the liberal order it has created.”
- “Innovation is not just about invention… production and diffusion is so widely important.”

## Contested / bridges

> **Contested altitude (2026-08-01):** Keyu’s account of managed US–China rivalry and a multipolar coalition of willing countries aligns with [[concepts/multipolar-balancing-and-asean]] as described by Chatib, the Bravo panel, and Gita. It conflicts with Jiang’s primary framing of an [[concepts/elite-overproduction|intra-elite civil war]]. Sources: [[sources/009-endgame-239-keyu-jin]], [[sources/006-endgame-216-chatib-basri]], [[sources/008-endgame-259-jiang-xueqin]].

> **Bridge (2026-08-01):** Innovation through better processes and wider diffusion, combined with affordable Chinese technology stacks, reinforces [[concepts/copy-with-pride-application-layer]]. It does not contradict the pursuit of exportable intellectual property through [[concepts/deep-tech-long-termism]] because the two strategies operate at different layers.

> **Bridge (2026-08-01):** Mayor economy is the mechanism inside [[concepts/decentralization-paradox]] (Tom #248 cited Keyu by name; this episode is the primary source).

## Open questions

- Student-exchange counts, manufacturing-share claims, the extent to which tariffs pass through into inflation, and the literature on incentives in “mayor tournaments” need corroboration.
- It remains unclear how far district-level competitions for firms generalize beyond the technology corridors of Beijing and Shanghai.
- It is also uncertain whether the “reluctance to create losers” is a durable shift or a cyclical feature of politics under Xi.
