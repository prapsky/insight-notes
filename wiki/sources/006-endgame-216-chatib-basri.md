## Summary

Chatib begins with his education and his accidental entry into economics. He then discusses LPDP scholarships and how to improve university culture at scale. His national-development argument has several parts. Indonesia faces a **[[concepts/middle-class-squeeze|middle-class squeeze]]**, similar to the Chile paradox in which policy helps the poor while the middle class lacks specific support. Limited monetary and government budgets leave **FDI (foreign direct investment) and legal certainty** as major sources of growth. Tax policy should improve **compliance and administration** before raising rates. Government must also adapt to Industry 4.0, balance relations through BRICS, OECD, and ASEAN, finance the energy transition realistically, and teach students to tell stories and ask better questions in the AI era.

## Core claims (attributed)

### Formation & education culture

1. **He became an economist by accident.** He wanted to study theater at the Jakarta Institute of the Arts, but his military father directed him toward a “real” profession. Reversing his priorities on the Sipenmaru university application placed him in the Faculty of Economics at the University of Indonesia. He coped by making mathematics human, for example by treating love as a regression. He later pursued master’s and doctoral study in Australia after impulsively dropping a UK English course when an Asian Development Bank scholarship arrived. He uses this story to show that people often act impulsively rather than as rationally as economic models assume.
2. **The LPDP scholarship program is necessary but not sufficient.** Chatib helped design the early program in 2013 while in government. More Indonesians now enter leading schools such as Harvard’s Kennedy School, but China and India actively prepare applicants for tests such as the SAT and GRE. LPDP should fund this preparation, not only tuition. ([[concepts/stem-talent-pipeline]])
3. **A strong campus culture requires a critical mass of serious participants.** High-quality discussion needs an audience that makes scholars read and prepare. Chatib says Iwan Jaya Azis moved to Cornell partly to find that audience. Giving diaspora members the right to work abroad can also build representation and networks. When about 88% of household heads lack a bachelor’s degree or higher, podcasts and other substantive media can partly provide the culture of public discussion that universities and politics do not reach.
4. **Universities should recruit the best faculty regardless of origin.** Singapore’s Lee Kuan Yew School uses its endowment to hire world-class teachers, including foreigners, while Indonesian universities often prefer their own alumni. Chatib wants universities to replace the supposedly realistic ambition of ranking around 150 with an aspiration to enter the top 20, supported by adequate funding and arrangements such as split appointments for diaspora scholars.
5. **Teachers who tell stories can draw students into difficult subjects.** A gifted storyteller may attract more students to physics than an expert who cannot communicate. Chatib therefore treats faculty quality as the main constraint on university quality. This aligns with [[concepts/soft-infrastructure-teachers]].

### Macro / investment / tax

6. **Trump’s policies could strengthen the dollar.** Deportations could reduce unskilled labor in the US and raise costs and inflation. Tax cuts could increase the deficit, require more bond sales, raise yields, make Federal Reserve rate cuts harder, and strengthen the dollar. Emerging-market central banks would then have less freedom. BI is also cautious because of the 1998 crisis. Indonesia’s budget room is narrow, with a tax ratio around 10–11% and about Rp800T in interest and principal payments in the interview’s 2025 framing.
7. **Structural reform and foreign direct investment are the remaining options.** Chatib jokes that many Indonesians become religious because dealing with government is so uncertain that one can only pray for permit approval. Vietnam shows a correlation between imports from China and exports to the United States, suggesting that production is being rerouted through Vietnam. Indonesia does not show the same pattern. It could benefit if it fixes its investment climate; otherwise it may only face pressure from dumped Chinese goods. Southeast Asia receives about $200 billion in foreign direct investment per year. Singapore receives $100–140 billion, while Indonesia receives $25–31 billion. An additional roughly $40 billion, bringing Indonesia to about $71 billion, could add about 3 percentage points of growth to an economy of about $1.4–1.5 trillion, while still leaving it below Singapore. Investors seek a credible **direction of change**, not perfection. ([[concepts/fdi-climate]], [[concepts/uncertainty-to-risk-translation]])
8. **Domestic savings cannot fund the required investment.** The incremental capital-output ratio, or ICOR, is about 6.6–6.8 and measures how much investment is needed to generate additional output. At that ratio, 7% growth requires investment equal to about 48% of gross domestic product. Domestic savings equal about 37%, so Indonesia must import capital. Broad money, known as M2, equals about 45% of gross domestic product and further limits domestic financing. Cross-link: [[concepts/money-supply-vs-interest-rates]].
9. **The tax ratio depends on compliance, not only rates.** Raising rates without broadening the tax base adds little revenue, while cutting rates does not broaden the base when compliance remains low. This is the World Bank framing. An *American Economic Review* paper with Ben Olken and Rema Hanna describes overloaded small tax offices called KPP Pratama. One account representative may handle about 4,000 taxpayers or 150 firms and therefore pursues only the top 10. Those firms may split themselves to avoid scrutiny. Moving the largest taxpayers to medium-sized KPP Madya offices can produce large revenue at low cost. The Ministry of Finance had added about 38 Madya offices from around 2022–23 and, Chatib says, should keep expanding. This should be paired with Coretax, cross-agency data through KOTI digital integration, and AI profiling. The unpaid tax gap is about 6.4 percentage points of gross domestic product, so successful execution could raise the tax ratio toward about 17%. ([[concepts/tax-compliance-and-administration]])

### Middle class, inequality, AI adjustment

10. **The middle class is being squeezed.** Its share rose from about 5% in 2003 to about 18% in 2014, peaked around 21–23%, and fell to about 17% in 2023. From 2019 to 2023, income still grew for the bottom 40%, though more slowly. Income fell for groups 5–8, while the top 20% gained about 4%. This resembles Milanovic’s “elephant curve.” Concert and iPhone spending mainly reflects the top group. The poor receive social protection and the rich benefit from growth, but much of the middle class pays little tax, with spending around Rp800k–9.9M, so tax relief does not help. It needs targeted measures such as electricity discounts for users at or below 2,200W. This is the Chile paradox described through Bachelet and Sebastian Edwards: there is no specific middle-class policy. ([[concepts/middle-class-squeeze]])
11. **Development and capital move toward the center.** Primary cities pull ahead of secondary cities. The internet, AI, and limits on bank branches can concentrate capital instead of democratizing it. Financial inclusion is not the same as making capital broadly available.
12. **The private sector should help workers learn new skills.** AI eliminates back-office and call-center jobs. The economy may absorb workers in the long run, but that does not solve the short-run adjustment. Government vocational training centers, known as BLK, often use obsolete tools. Chatib offers Citibank’s Executive Development Program as a model and supports a **double tax deduction** for private training, as well as industrial policy for AI skills.
13. **Government 4.0 deserves more concern than Industry 4.0.** Technical rules can become obsolete within months, while laws cannot change as quickly. Chatib argues that policymakers should agree on principles, such as inclusion and consumer protection, rather than prescribing details such as USB specifications. As he puts it, “Agile bureaucracy is an oxymoron.” ([[concepts/government-4-0-and-agile-regulation]])
14. **AI is capital-intensive engineering, not merely software.** Operating at scale requires large investments in infrastructure such as data centers, and the leading positions belong to large firms in the United States and China. Indonesia must connect itself to capital networks, persuasive narratives, and nearby diaspora expertise instead of watching from the sidelines.

### Geopolitics & energy

15. **Indonesia should balance among several powers.** It should avoid being forced into a binary choice between the United States and China, including over an Internet of Things technology stack. Joining both BRICS and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development can support that balance. ASEAN should act as one entity through arrangements such as the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership instead of falling into a bilateral trap; Gita is skeptical of the Trans-Pacific Partnership. Chatib proposes proactive cooperation among the United States, China, and Indonesia in digital technology, energy, and critical minerals. Because Chinese firms own most Indonesian nickel production, access to the US market is a problem. India’s ability to project soft power in many directions provides a model. Storytelling and soft power are strategic assets. ([[concepts/multipolar-balancing-and-asean]])
16. **Economic security is replacing pure efficiency as an organizing principle.** Supply chains are moving toward “just in case” resilience and friend-shoring. In advanced economies, foreign policy increasingly leads and economics follows, but Chatib considers Indonesia weak at this game. Bilateral arrangements are also becoming more important as multilateralism frays.
17. **Financing the energy transition requires domestic and international tools.** Installed capacity is about 80 gigawatts, while electricity use is about 1,300 kilowatt-hours per person and much higher in peer countries. Modernizing toward about 6,000 kilowatt-hours per person while supporting AI would take about a century if capacity grows by only 3 gigawatts per year. Scaling generation could cost roughly $1 trillion, approximately the size of gross domestic product, at about $2 million per megawatt, with renewables costing more. Domestic policy can phase out fossil fuels and impose a carbon tax. International finance can include concessional loans, special drawing rights from the International Monetary Fund, blended finance, and risk reduction that attracts private capital on the scale of BlackRock or Vanguard. Accreditation by multilateral institutions matters, while US withdrawal under Trump creates risk. **Incentives cannot substitute for legal certainty.**

### Politics of the right / closing

18. **Inequality helps drive politics toward the right.** Campaigns based on fear and intimidation are easier to sustain than campaigns based on hope because hope requires credibility. In a framing Gita attributes to a Fukuyama panel, neoliberalism failed to distribute capital fairly, while “woke” politics mistakes visible outcomes for their deeper causes. Globalization has changed from a story of prosperity into a source of uncertainty and conflict. Social media privileges emotion over objective data, and Chatib invokes Jeffrey Frieden’s argument that inequality increases demand for immediate identity-based and nationalist answers.
19. **Education in the AI era should teach students to ask better questions.** Exams can require students to formulate questions that ChatGPT cannot answer well and then develop those questions into research. Critical thinking and clear expression also provide a foundation for soft power.
20. **Older generations should not judge Generation Z only through inherited standards.** Young people should learn from earlier generations, including their mistakes, and should filter information to avoid echo chambers. Chatib warns that postmodern culture often favors opinion over fact and argues for restoring a stronger factual basis.

## Notable quotes / framings

- On permits: “One of the reasons many Indonesians become religious is because they have to deal with the government” — uncertainty so high you only pray for approval.
- On growth: when monetary and government budget room are limited, “solusinya hanya FDI.” Focus on the letter **I**, meaning investment.
- On middle class: governments focus on the poor then the rich enjoy development — “gak ada spesifik kebijakan untuk yang middle class.”
- On regulation: “Agile bureaucracy is an oxymoron.”
- On incentives vs certainty: you cannot substitute incentives for legal certainty.
- On AI-era schooling: train people to ask questions, not only give answers AI already supplies.

## Biography thread (compressed)

Chatib was born in Jakarta to Minang parents. His father worked around the secretary-general level of the Ministry of Manpower and retired as a major general. His literary uncle, Asrul Sani, provided a counterweight to military strictness. Chatib attended Kanisius and reluctantly entered the Faculty of Economics at the University of Indonesia. After abandoning a UK path, an Asian Development Bank scholarship connected through Peter McCawley took him to the Australian National University for master’s and doctoral study. After serving in the Ministry of Finance, he became a visiting fellow at Harvard. He has worked as a teacher and public intellectual, published tax-administration research with Olken and Hanna in the *American Economic Review*, helped design LPDP, and discussed the middle class, foreign direct investment, and geopolitics on Endgame #216.
