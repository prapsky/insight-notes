## Summary

The conversation begins with Tom’s education, family, and intellectual development on Wall Street. It then turns to nation-building. Tom emphasizes **soft infrastructure** (the people and institutions that help society work), especially teachers. He argues that serious media and civil society can change culture. He discusses the different ways China and democracies decentralize power. He also explains how permits and corruption reinforce each other, and proposes an [[concepts/ownership-society|ownership society]] as one response. Other themes include [[concepts/risk-as-innovation-fuel|risk as innovation fuel]], his spiritual development after prison and abolition, and [[concepts/idea-contest-and-centrism|centrism via open contest of ideas]].

## Core claims (attributed)

### Character & culture

1. **Success needs ambition, imagination, and luck.** The word “ambisi” often sounds negative in Indonesia, but Tom thinks that should change. Policy often fails because leaders reuse the same methods and lack imagination, vision, or clear communication. Luck is real, and humility and openness make people more ready to receive it.
2. **Character matters more than credentials alone.** He associates his father with intellect, ambition, and arrogance, and his mother with heart and conscience. He warns that extreme capitalism can “make us sell our soul.” As a parent, he rejects strict “tiger parenting” and lets his children choose their own paths.
3. **Social virtue matters more than ritual virtue alone.** Character appears in small acts of respect toward staff, waiters, and security workers. Nation-building needs a culture of empathy, not only religious performance.
4. **Serious media can change culture.** Podcasters and influencers who focus on substance rather than entertainment are creating demand for trusted educational content. He compares this with the printing press developing from pamphlets into magazines. Changes in culture and mindset come before changes in politics and economics.

### Soft infrastructure & governance

5. **Teachers are the most important soft infrastructure.** No country becomes advanced without investing in people and institutions. About 20% of the national budget goes to education, but too little may reach teacher pay, contract-teacher allowances, and teaching skills. Too much may go to vocational programs with clear job outcomes that could instead be **funded through credit**. ([[concepts/soft-infrastructure-teachers|Soft infrastructure / teachers]])
6. **Decentralization needs central guidance.** About 70% of the education budget is spread across more than 500 local points, while ministries use different teacher-recruitment criteria. Tom wants to keep decentralization but add national-to-regional performance measures, benchmarks, and an “academy” that teaches local leaders how to govern well. ([[concepts/decentralization-paradox|Decentralization paradox]])
7. **China combines central politics with local economic competition.** Many democracies do the reverse. Beijing still responds to public opinion, and party officials compete on merit. In his view, China does not replace accountability with authority. He presents the United Kingdom’s “London-centric” structure as a warning about slow growth.
8. **Populism eventually loses energy.** Extreme movements on the left and right run out of fuel when anger becomes exhausting and they fail to improve shared welfare. People then move back toward the center-left or center-right. He dates the current wave roughly to Brexit and Trump in 2016. ([[concepts/idea-contest-and-centrism|Idea contest & centrism]])
9. **Ideas should compete openly.** People should debate without fear, accept disagreement, and avoid treating criticism as a personal attack. Without testing through debate, bad ideas can become policy by decree.

### Licensing, capital, risk

10. **Gita names five features behind China’s growth compared with Southeast Asia.** They are hard infrastructure, education, a link between talent and power, **permits**, and economic decentralization. The cited permit figures are about 10 per 1,000 adults in China, about 1 in Southeast Asia, and about 0.3 in Indonesia and the Philippines.
11. **Licensing reflects money, business culture, and bureaucracy.** In a corrupt system, officials have little reason to remove permit steps because each step can generate rent (private income from control). Tom suggests better public funding for political parties, including an Anies-aligned idea that much of their cost should come from the national budget. He also favors long-term ownership stakes that become more valuable when policy improves. ([[concepts/ownership-society|Ownership society]])
12. **An ownership society can change incentives.** In advanced economies, much prosperity comes from rising asset values rather than wages. Officials should benefit more from honest, long-term asset growth than from short-term rent extraction. This is his net present value, or NPV, argument, which compares the value of benefits received at different times.
13. **FDI depends on trust.** Like Purbaya, Tom names two foundations: rule of law and the ability to turn uncertainty into risks that investors can price. Singapore and Switzerland attract capital because investors trust them. Cross-link: [[concepts/fdi-climate]], [[concepts/uncertainty-to-risk-translation]].
14. **Risk is fuel, not only danger.** After the 1997 and 2008 crises, policymakers tried too hard to remove risk and weakened innovation. Society should accept failure, protect people through bankruptcy law, and avoid criminalizing honest business losses when there was no criminal intent. ([[concepts/risk-as-innovation-fuel|Risk as innovation fuel]])

### Personal arc (brief)

15. **Prison, abolition, and surrender to God.** He was detained for about 9 months, received a 4.5-year sentence, and then received abolition. He says this was only about the 7th or 8th abolition in the republic’s history. He sees the experience as part of a divine plan: make an effort, then accept the outcome. It made social virtue and humility into lived lessons, grounded in an experience of injustice rather than doctrine alone.

## Notable quotes / framings

- On soft infra: “Enggak ada bangsa yang maju tanpa adanya investasi di infrastruktur lunak.”
- On China: “Bulat enggak mau mensubstitusi accountability… dengan otoritas.”
- On populism: extreme movements “ujungnya kehabisan bensin” when they fail to create lasting, shared welfare.
- On risk: criminalizing an ordinary business failure leads to the question, “It’s a crime to fail in business?” This is the opposite of Silicon Valley’s acceptance of failure.
- On culture change: serious podcasts first change mindsets and culture. Politics and economic strategy follow.

## Biography thread (compressed)

Tom’s father was a physician and intellectual who studied medicine at the University of Indonesia and then specialized in Germany. His mother had literary and musical interests and played the piano. Tom lived in Germany from about age three to ten, attended schools in Jakarta, and then went to Deerfield Academy, a preparatory school that sends students to leading universities. He entered Harvard intending to study architecture but became fascinated by Wall Street, including JP Morgan’s role as a crisis backstop before the Federal Reserve existed. He built a career in finance and later gained experience with licensing at the Investment Coordinating Board, or BKPM, alongside Gita. He joined Anies’s presidential-campaign circle, where the ownership-society concept developed. After criminalization, prison, and abolition, he renewed his spiritual practice.
