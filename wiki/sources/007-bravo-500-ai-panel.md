## Summary

Gita begins with the gap between Indonesia’s AI ambitions and its limited electricity supply. The panel then offers three views. Former policymaker Budi discusses Jensen Huang’s five-layer AI stack, power wheeling (sending privately generated electricity through PLN’s grid), regulation based on principles, and focused LPDP scholarships. Google Cloud operator Sai separates physical sovereignty from cryptographic sovereignty (controlling data through encryption and rules). He also discusses AI agents, computing spread across different devices, regulatory test environments, and Southeast Asia’s ability to use both US and Chinese technology. Conglomerate builder Franky describes investment in power and data centers, adapting proven applications through “copy with pride,” and treating people as the main asset. Hub concepts: [[concepts/ai-electrification-threshold]], [[concepts/physical-vs-cryptographic-sovereignty]], [[concepts/copy-with-pride-application-layer]].

## Core claims (attributed)

### Backdrop — energy as AI precondition (Gita)

1. **Indonesia has a large power-capacity gap.** China generates about 4,000 GW, the US about 1,400 GW, and Indonesia about 100 GW. China and the US use about 10,000 kWh per person. In Southeast Asia, only Singapore and Brunei reach that level. Indonesia uses about 1,300 kWh per person.
2. **An AI-capable country uses about 10,000 kilowatt-hours per person.** Gita’s camp argues that nations need roughly this level of electricity use to become AI *creators*, rather than only consumers or enablers. In his framing, a query to a large language model uses about 10–50 times as much energy as a Google search.
3. **The arithmetic of catching up produces a very long timeline.** Raising Indonesia to about 10,000 kilowatt-hours per person would require roughly **600 gigawatts**, or about seven times its current power capacity. At a construction rate of about 5 gigawatts per year, the simple calculation gives about **120 years**. Gita also cites about 50,000 terabytes of data per day and about 200 million cyberattacks per day. These figures are stated in the interview and remain unverified here.
4. This argument connects with the realistic view of energy-transition financing in [[sources/006-endgame-216-chatib-basri|Chatib #216]], which cites a similar figure of about 1,300 kilowatt-hours per person and a construction timeline measured in centuries. See [[concepts/ai-electrification-threshold]].

### Jensen stack & power wheeling (Budi)

5. **Jensen Huang’s framing has five layers.** They are (1) energy, (2) chips, (3) infrastructure, including data centers and graphics processing units, (4) models, and (5) applications. Budi concentrates his concern on the first three layers and considers Indonesia’s talent adequate for the fourth and fifth.
6. **A proposal for power wheeling was blocked.** This was a limited deregulation of electricity law, not privatization. It would have allowed hydroelectric generators to supply data-center customers by leasing capacity on PLN’s grid, thereby bypassing bureaucratic and technical bottlenecks. The government and parliament rejected the proposal. The grid remains heavily dependent on coal even though data-center customers want green power.
7. **Advanced chips are another constraint.** About 90% of advanced chip supply passes through TSMC. Building data centers with graphics processors therefore requires direct access to Taiwan or access through a partner.
8. **Power-plant lead times are becoming longer.** A one-gigawatt plant now takes about **7 years** to reach its *ready for service* date, often abbreviated RFS, compared with about four years a decade ago. Indonesia has a ten-year electricity supply plan called the RUPTL, but today’s small data-center and graphics-processor capacity does not provide a path to hyperscale capacity.
9. **Competitive sectors need regulation based on principles.** Sectors such as smart handsets need broad principles rather than detailed rules, while monopolies can remain governed by detailed rules. This aligns with [[concepts/government-4-0-and-agile-regulation]]. Budi applies Mark Cuban’s framing to countries: there will be countries that use AI and “former countries” that failed to do so.
10. **Budi hopes the cabinet understands AI.** He became optimistic after the late-2024 cabinet induction. The automatic speech recognition transcript names the facilitator as “Michael Houseman,” but that identity is uncertain. Budi wants light-touch cooperation between private companies and government.

### Sovereignty under constraint (Sai)

11. **Physical and cryptographic sovereignty are different.** Physical sovereignty means local data centers, models, and services. Cryptographic sovereignty means tracking data that leaves the country and enforcing rules about how it may be used. Policy often waits for visible local infrastructure. Sai argues that cryptographic control is the practical near-term path while power and advanced chips remain scarce. See [[concepts/physical-vs-cryptographic-sovereignty]].
12. **Indonesia lacks mid-level talent that can put AI into production.** The economy often treats AI as a collection of science projects. The missing step is making it mainstream through mid-level operators who can standardize systems within regulatory boundaries and use *existing* infrastructure more effectively.
13. **Agentic AI uses distributed computing and more energy.** An agent repeatedly perceives information, processes it, plans, and executes actions. This iterative cycle uses disproportionately more energy than generative chat. Sai expects computing to be chosen for each purpose, whether in the cloud, on a laptop, or on a mobile device. A remote clinic with little bandwidth is one example. Policymakers should begin with the desired outcome and work backward instead of waiting until the world has enough data-center power. In his Mythos anecdote, visibility tools revealed a 27-year-old vulnerability that had remained “in front of our face.”
14. **Technology grows exponentially while regulators learn more linearly.** Sai supports government AI sandboxes like Singapore’s and Google’s digital-literacy work with the Ministry of Education and other ministries. Financial services, healthcare, and retail all face a gap between generating ideas and putting them into production. Organizations should start with today’s technology rather than waiting for technology and regulation to become ready at the same time. Using a software-development metaphor, they should build a “hello world” example and then add safeguards. In one hacking anecdote, the best detection time fell from about 30 minutes to about 15 seconds.
15. **Open models can support primary-care triage.** Sai points to MedGemma and work with the Ministry of Health as examples of an available technology stack. Such tools could help triage patients locally so that people in Papua, Sulawesi, or Kalimantan do not always have to travel to a single specialist hub.

### Conglomerate stack & copy-with-pride (Franky)

16. **Sinarmas participates across the digital-infrastructure stack.** Its activities include coal, geothermal, and solar power, as well as land and data centers. Franky says that more than **40 megawatts** of capacity had been taken at the time of the panel, while the market was discussing 100–200 megawatts. He presents these investments as a way to reduce the government’s burden of adding generation capacity.
17. **Franky does not expect an Indonesian DeepSeek soon.** He says Indonesia is starting late and would need talent, government support, and substantial capital that it could afford to spend. He considers the chances “not big” and prefers adopting a platform and localizing its application layers rather than reinventing a foundation model. See [[concepts/copy-with-pride-application-layer]].
18. **“Copy with pride” means adapting proven systems.** Indonesia can adopt Chinese education, healthcare, and industrial platforms and modify them for Indonesian needs. The interview claims accuracy of about 95–97% in some healthcare subjects compared with an average doctor. Indonesia can compete at the application layer. China may face restrictions that slow some technologies, but it leads in applications, scale, attitude, and government support.
19. **Franky treats people as the only asset.** Indonesia should retain about 250,000 graduates in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics, import mentors, and pay more so talent does not leave for Singapore. The main challenges are discomfort during transformation and choosing solutions that are both robust and safe. Human beings retain wisdom while AI takes repetitive work. He cites a China visit in which a network operations center, or NOC, reduced staffing from 500 people to 10.
20. **Sovereign data is not the same as a sovereign large language model.** Indonesia needs sovereign *control* over its data, and it can achieve that control in several ways without building another DeepSeek.

### Talent, education, geopolitics (cross-panel)

21. **STEM graduates need a place to apply their skills.** Budi says even 250,000 graduates in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics will leave if they have no field in which to deliver results, and Singapore will absorb them. Conglomerates that combine the full AI system, from power to data centers to operations, are rare. Indonesia needs policy “playgrounds” where talent can work, not only slogans about jobs. Cross-link [[concepts/stem-talent-pipeline]].
22. **Gita describes four models for managing national talent.** Japan emphasizes training people at home, Australia gains talent through immigration, China circulates talent by sending more than about 9 million people abroad and drawing many back, and India maintains links with a diaspora of more than about 7 million people. Countries should manage these approaches as a portfolio. See [[concepts/brain-gain-and-circulation]].
23. **Budi favors directing LPDP scholarships toward long-term national needs.** While in government, he restricted scholarships under his authority to the digital economy and emphasized three destinations: the San Francisco Bay Area, Beijing and Tsinghua, and Bangalore. He did not want an unrestricted menu of MBA programs. In his view, the national LPDP program should focus on the capabilities and institutional brands Indonesia will need in 10–20 years.
24. **Southeast Asia should preserve the option to work with several technology systems.** The region has about 700 million people and roughly $4 trillion in gross domestic product. Gita and Sai argue that it should not be forced to choose only the United States or China. Sai says countries should select the best available technology. AI is only about **10%** of the problem, much as an engine is only one part of an airline; branding, operations, customers, and safety remain local. Chinese and Western companies may coexist, so policymakers should not focus too heavily on a split into two camps. This reinforces [[concepts/multipolar-balancing-and-asean]].
25. **Education policy must work on a 10–20-year horizon.** Budi cites Singapore’s coding buses for preschool children as an example and says Indonesia cannot depend on government alone. Private institutions, including those run by Sinarmas, should align curricula with capabilities the country will need in 10–20 years. Sai expects more personalized learning: institutions provide knowledge and purpose, while AI extends creativity beyond school. Gita says about 88% of household heads across Indonesia lack a university education, so teachers and role models must cultivate ambition, imagination, and practiced serendipity. Franky adds that the private sector must both educate people and **pay** enough to retain them. This argument connects with [[concepts/soft-infrastructure-teachers]].

## Notable quotes / framings

- Gita contrasts Indonesia’s desire for modernity with its construction rate. Multiplying capacity about sevenfold while adding only about 5 gigawatts per year would take “120 years… unless we’re taking stem cell injections.”
- Budi applies Mark Cuban’s comparison to nations: there will be companies and countries that use AI, and there will be *former* companies and countries.
- Sai argues that Indonesia should stop waiting for a “big bang” of local hyperscale infrastructure. It should establish cryptographic control now and improve it over time.
- On geopolitics, Sai says, “I wouldn’t be too hung up on which engine I want to fit into my plane.”
- On foundation models, Franky says, “I don’t want to be another DeepSeek, and I don’t think I can.”
- Their shared closing message is to act now: Budi says to maximize AI use, Sai says there has never been a better time, and Franky says to follow the major trend while retaining talent.
